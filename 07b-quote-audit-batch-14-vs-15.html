Quote audit — batches 14 and 15

The check that located the fabrication. Each rater's coding was matched against the source transcripts, conversation by conversation. Batch 14 (sound) and batch 15 (fabricated) are shown side by side for both raters.

During blind coding, each of the two raters processed the corpus in fifteen sequential batches. In the fifteenth and final batch, both produced fluent, correctly formatted coding for five conversations whose transcripts were present and pasted correctly — but what they coded was not what was in front of them. The audit below is the evidence. For every conversation, a cited anchor from the rater's coding was checked against the source: does the quote appear in the transcript (In source?), and does the conversation the rater described match the real one under that number (Title matches?).

Read the two batches together. In batch 14 every anchor is found in the source and every conversation is the real one — and the two raters, coding independently, happen to cite different genuine lines from the same conversations, which is what a real transcript yields. In batch 15 every anchor fails, and for the same conversation number each rater invented a different scenario. Identical, correctly pasted input produced different inventions — which locates the failure in the raters, not the corpus.

On language. The corpus is mostly Hebrew. Titles and quotes that were originally in Hebrew are shown here in English translation for readability; quotes that were originally in English appear as written. The verbatim match reported under In source? was performed against the original-language transcripts, not the translations. The original-language source library is available from the author on request.

Conversation 28, side by side. Both raters were given the same input under the same number, and its real title — a woman coping with feeling unseen by her husband and a cycle of disappointment–anger–silence — is identical in both. Yet Claude Sonnet 4.6 coded a continuation about a husband after their son's cancer diagnosis, and Claude Fable 5 coded a mother deciding how to answer an estranged son's request to meet. Two different invented conversations, two different fabricated quotes, neither in the source. In batch 14, by contrast, the two raters also cite different lines — but there every line is real and found in the transcript. Divergent invention from identical input is the signature that places the failure in the raters.

The quote check caught this because fabrication leaves a trace: a cited quote that is not in the source. Run per batch as the coding proceeds — rather than after the fact — the same check flags the failure as it happens. This excerpt shows the decisive contrast (batch 14 vs. batch 15); the full audit covers batches 8 through 15 on both ends of the run.

Methodology appendix to Pulling the Sycophancy Thread. Conversations were coded in their original language (mostly Hebrew); the two raters were independent models — Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Fable 5 — coding blind against the frozen rubric. Verdicts are reproduced from each rater's coding; Hebrew titles and anchors are translated into English here, and the verbatim match was checked against the original-language source.
